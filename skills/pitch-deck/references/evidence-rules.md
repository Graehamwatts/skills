# Evidence rules

Read this before any number, ranking word, competitor name, price or ask reaches a slide. Read section 13 again before calling a deck final.

Every company, person and figure in the examples below is invented. Never carry an example number into a real deck.

## 1. Why these rules come first

A reader cannot check everything in a deck, so they sample. They test the one claim they happen to know something about and judge the rest by it. One stretched number costs the founder every other number on the page.

An invented number is also a trap for the founder. Someone will ask about it in the first meeting, and the founder will have no answer, because they never knew where it came from.

A model writing a deck is the likeliest source of an invented number. A slide template has slots that expect figures, and a slot invites a figure. So the rule is mechanical: a number goes on a slide only when it is one of the three kinds in section 4. Everything else is a placeholder that says what is missing.

None of this makes a deck timid. A deck built from the founder's real numbers reads as specific. A deck built from safe generalities reads as generic. The cure for generic is real numbers, real screens, a real price and a real ask.

## 2. The facts sheet

Before the outline, write one file of canonical facts. Call it `facts.md` and keep it beside the deck outline, in the founder's own project folder. It holds private numbers, so it never goes into this skill's folder or a public repository. Every number in every version of the deck must match it.

It holds:

- What the company makes and for whom, in one sentence.
- The start date, and who does the building.
- The stage in exact words. Write revenue to date as a figure, "$0" included. The slide will not dwell on it, but the sheet must be exact so that nothing downstream blurs it.
- Customers and users, each count tagged with its kind of proof (section 5) and its dates.
- The price or price range, and whether the founder has decided it.
- Cost to serve one customer for a month.
- The raise: amount, instrument, milestone, runway in months, use of funds. Mark each as decided or open.
- Every outside source used, with publisher, title, year, link and the date it was read.

Three rules keep it honest.

**The founder owns it.** It is their record. Add to it, never silently rewrite it.

**The newest thing the founder says wins.** When they contradict an entry, update the entry and tell them which fact was replaced.

**A conflict stops the work.** If two documents give two numbers for the same thing, do not pick one. Ask.

## 3. The claims ledger

A founder describes the product they see in their head, which is partly built and partly planned. The ledger separates the two before a slide can blur them. Write it before any slide.

One row per capability the founder describes:

| Capability, in the founder's words | Status | Evidence | How the company's own documents describe it | Conflict or note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sends payment reminders by text | Runs today | Used it; screenshot on file | Product guide, "Reminders" page | None |
| Calls the customer after three texts | Being built | Spec dated last month; no working version seen | Spec says a person approves each call | The pitch says "fully automatic". The spec says a person approves. |
| Matches payments to the accounting ledger | Later | Founder's roadmap note | Not in any document | Keep off the product slides |
| Emails the owner a weekly summary | Stated, not checked | The founder's note says "it will email the owner" | Nothing found | Their own tense is future. Ask |

Fill the evidence column by looking: open the product, read the documentation, read the site, read the specification and the earlier decks. The ledger often finds places where the pitch runs against the company's own written rules. Raise each one with the founder. Do not settle it by choosing the more impressive version.

**Record the founder's own tense.** "Will", "should be able to", "in theory" and "imagine" in their notes or dictation are evidence that a capability does not run yet, whatever tense an outline or an earlier deck uses.

**Use the names in the company's current documents.** Products and features get renamed while the founder goes on saying the old name out loud. If the dictation and the documents differ, ask which name is public.

Status decides the tense.

- **Runs today.** Present tense. A real screen. A result may be attached if it was measured. "Tallybird texts the customer."
- **Being built.** Show it as what the product is made to do: "Tallybird is built to call after three texts." Its screen carries the small label "Sample data". It sits under Now or Next on the dated strip. No result, no time saving and no customer is attached to it.
- **Later.** It belongs in the Next column of the strip or in the appendix. It does not appear on a product slide.
- **Stated, not checked.** The founder or an outline says it, and nobody has opened the product. This is the honest status when the work starts from pasted text. Treat it as being built until it is checked: no present-tense result, no time saving, no customer attached. Send these rows to the founder as a three-way pick: used on a real case and since when, being built, or later.

A "Sample data" label covers a screen. It does not cover a sentence or a tick. A present-tense line, or a tick in a comparison, for something the deck's own strip lists under Now or Next is present tense for what does not run.

The ledger is a working file. The deck does not print it and does not mark features with status badges. A deck with "runs today" or "in build" stamped beside each feature reads as a confession, and founders reject it for good reason. The ledger keeps the deck truthful without making the deck about its own gaps.

## 4. Three kinds of number, and the placeholder

| Kind | What it is | What goes on file | How it shows on the slide |
|---|---|---|---|
| The founder's own | From their records or their own use of the product | Who said it, when, and which record it comes from | Plain, with its time window: "310 invoices, March to August" |
| Sourced | From an outside publisher | Publisher, title, year, link, date read | A small source line under it: publisher, title, year |
| Estimate | Calculated from other numbers | Each input, which is itself the founder's or sourced, and the arithmetic | The single word "Estimate" in small type. The working goes in the notes or the appendix |

Anything that is none of these is missing. Write it in the draft as a placeholder that names what belongs there and who can supply it:

`[NEED: monthly price per shop, founder to decide]`

A placeholder can stand for any missing fact, not only a figure: a real case, a reason, a name, a decision.

`[NEED: the one thing that is hard for an established company, founder]`

Collect every placeholder at the end of the draft under the heading **Numbers to find**, so the founder sees in one place what they owe. Placeholders are welcome in a draft and banned in a final file (section 13).

More rules for every number:

- **A unit and a time window.** A bare "18K" means nothing. Recurring revenue is not total revenue. Say which, and say for what period.
- **Prefer the founder's number to a survey's.** One real figure from their own operation beats three statistics about the industry.
- **Few numbers.** Each one costs the reader attention. Keep the ones the argument needs.
- **Numbers stay as text.** A figure that exists only inside a chart image cannot be searched, copied or read by a screening tool.
- **A drawn or mocked screen carries "Sample data".** A real screen beats a sample one. A deck with only sample screens reads as a concept.

## 5. Kinds of proof, never upgraded

Label every proof point by its kind. Use the highest kind that is true, say which it is, and never word a lower kind as a higher one.

1. Paying customers with no tie to the founder.
2. Paying customers who know the founder.
3. Outside users who use it and do not pay yet.
4. The founder's own operation running on the product, with a count from real use.
5. A pilot agreed in writing, with dates.
6. A signed letter of intent, with its value and terms.
7. A waitlist or signups.
8. Conversations.

"Customers" means they pay. "Users" means they use it. A pilot is not usage until it starts. A letter is not revenue. A waitlist count cannot carry a proof slide alone. Kinds 7 and 8 are leads, and a reader discounts them to nearly nothing.

Never claim customers, revenue, users, partnerships or results that do not exist. This is the one rule in the skill with no exceptions.

## 6. Ranking words and the prior-art check

Ranking words: first, only, best, leading, largest, fastest. Category crowns do the same job: "the operating system for", "all-in-one", "the platform for". Each is a claim about every other company in the world, which the founder almost never has the evidence to make.

A universal negative is the same claim with the ranking word taken out: "no other tool does this", "competitors cannot", "nobody else", "every other app stops at the reminder", "find me another". It still says something about every rival. Run the same check.

Run this check before any of them is written:

1. Restate the claim so it can be tested. "The only invoice tool for contractors" becomes "no other product sends payment reminders for trade contractors".
2. Search for it. Search the category, search the phrase itself, and read the product pages of the five to eight nearest alternatives, incumbents and adjacent tools included.
3. Record what was searched, which pages were read and on what date, and what turned up.
4. Expect to find the claim already taken. Competitors' own pages usually say the same thing in nearly the same words.
5. Narrow it. Ask what specific thing none of the products you read was doing. That narrower line is the one to use, and it rarely needs the ranking word: "Tallybird texts the customer from the shop's own phone number."
6. If the search finds nothing, that is an hour of looking, not proof. Keep the ranking word off the slide anyway. State what the product does and let the reader conclude it is unusual. Put "we found no product that does this, searched on [date]" in the notes for the question period.

A picture can make a ranking claim too. A grid with the company alone in the best corner is one. So is a table where the company has every tick.

## 7. Statements about competitors

- **Source: the competitor's own pages.** Their site, pricing page, documentation, help center or changelog, with the link and the date read. Review sites and forum posts are leads to follow up, not sources.
- **A summary is a lead, not a source.** A search engine's or an assistant's summary of a page is not the page. If the competitor's own page will not open, record that it was blocked, and write the statement as a question or leave it out. Say in the notes which pages were opened and which were only seen in results.
- **Use today's name.** Products get renamed and folded into others, and an imported outline often carries the old name. Check every product and company name on its owner's own page, with the date read.
- **Facts, not verdicts.** "Lists email reminders on its pricing page; no text option listed, read on [date]" is a fact. "Clunky and outdated" is a verdict.
- **Names go in the appendix.** The main run talks about kinds of alternative. The appendix holds the named table, each row with its source and date.
- **Place them where their own page puts them.** If a competitor describes itself the way the founder describes their own company, it sits where its page says it sits, on any map or in any table.
- **Pages change.** Re-read the sources before a deck goes out if the notes are more than a few weeks old.

## 8. Market arithmetic

The market is a sum the founder can say out loud in one breath: the number of customers, times what each pays in a year.

Invented example:

> 110,000 small trade shops x $240 a month x 12 = about $317 million a year.
> We start with 6,200 plumbing shops in two states: 6,200 x $240 x 12 = about $17.9 million a year.

In a real deck the shop counts would be sourced numbers with a source line. Here they are invented. The price is the founder's own. The product of the two is an estimate and is labeled as one.

Name the first segment, then say what comes after it. A reader wants to see where the company starts, not only where it could end.

Reject these on sight:

- A total lifted from an analyst report with no working.
- "If we capture just 1% of the market." It is not a plan, and every reader has seen it.
- Three nested rings with no arithmetic behind them.
- Neighboring markets added in to make the figure larger.
- Money that flows through the customer's industry, presented as money the customer would pay the company.
- A figure with no year on it.

Until a price exists, show two separate facts: how many customers there are, and what each spends today on the thing the product replaces. Do not multiply them into a market figure that nobody published.

## 9. Price against cost to serve

A price on a slide is a promise about the business. Check it against what one customer costs to serve before it is printed.

Build the cost for one customer for one month from real bills: usage-based services, hosting, payment fees, support time. Do it twice, for a typical customer and a heavy one.

Invented example. An imported outline proposed "$49 a month, unlimited":

| Cost per shop per month | Typical shop | Heavy shop |
|---|---|---|
| Messages sent | $9 | $45 |
| Usage-billed services | $17 | $85 |
| Payment support | $4 | $4 |
| Support time | $12 | $12 |
| **Total** | **$42** | **$146** |

At $49 the typical shop leaves $7 a month, a margin of about 14%. The heavy shop loses $97 a month. The price does not hold, and "unlimited" makes it worse, because one customer's usage can be several times another's.

At a base of $240 a month the typical shop leaves $198, a margin of about 82%, and heavy usage is charged as paid extra usage above a set allowance.

What follows from this:

- **A flat unlimited price cannot hold when usage costs swing widely.** Use a base price with paid extra usage.
- **Free credits are a cost, not margin.** A cost covered by a vendor's credits comes back when the credits end.
- **The price is the founder's decision.** If it is not set, bring a range: the floor comes from cost to serve, the ceiling from what the customer pays today for what the product replaces. Ask the founder to pick.
- **No inputs, no range.** If the bills and the replaced spend are not on file, ask for both in one question: the bills behind one customer's monthly cost, and what the customer pays today for what the product replaces. Do not build a range from an imported outline's figures, or from competitors' price pages alone.
- **Never print an invented tier, and never print "pricing to be decided".** One is a fabrication. The other tells the reader the founder does not know.

Keep the working on file and in the appendix. The slide shows the price and what it replaces.

## 10. The ask arithmetic

An ask has to agree with itself and with the rest of the deck. Run three checks.

1. **Milestone revenue.** Customers at the milestone, times the stated price, times twelve, equals the yearly revenue the slide claims.
2. **Monthly spend.** The amount divided by the months of runway is a monthly spend the plan can explain.
3. **Use of funds.** The lines add up to the amount.

Invented example that passes:

> Raising $900,000 to reach 150 paying shops in 18 months.
> Check 1: 150 x $240 x 12 = $432,000 a year. The slide says $432,000.
> Check 2: $900,000 / 18 = $50,000 a month.
> Check 3: building the product $430,000 + reaching customers $290,000 + running the company $180,000 = $900,000.

Invented example that fails, from an imported outline:

> "Raising $1.2 million to reach 200 shops and $1 million in annual revenue within 12 months. Use of funds: 40% product, 35% sales, 20% operations."
> Check 1: 200 x $240 x 12 = $576,000, which is $424,000 short of the claim. Reaching $1 million from 200 shops needs about $417 a month from each. At $240 it takes 348 shops.
> Check 2: $1.2 million / 12 = $100,000 a month. The outline names two hires and no other spending, so the plan cannot explain that rate.
> Check 3: 40 + 35 + 20 = 95. Five percent is unaccounted for.

When a check fails, one of the numbers has to change, and the founder chooses which. Show them the arithmetic and the options. Do not quietly adjust a number to make the sum work.

Two more points:

- **The starting count must be true.** "From 12 pilot shops to 200" asserts that there are 12 pilot shops today. That is a claim under section 5.
- **The same price everywhere.** The price on the business model slide, in the market sum and in the ask must be one number.

This skill checks arithmetic. It does not advise on valuation, instrument or terms. Those belong to the founder and their lawyer.

## 11. Charts that do not flatter

- Bars showing revenue or growth start at zero.
- No cumulative-only chart presented as growth. A cumulative line always goes up.
- A growth percentage sits beside the absolute number it grew from. Large growth on a tiny base misleads.
- Every metric carries its date or period.
- A projection is labeled as a projection, with the three to five assumptions that drive it visible beside it.
- The chart's conclusion is written in words above it. That line is the headline.

## 12. Imported outlines and numbers a reviewer proposes

Founders often arrive with an outline or a "deck prompt" from another AI, or with a review that proposes figures. Treat both as structure and suggestion, never as a source. An outline with no slides is handled in Import mode (`SKILL.md`), which uses the procedure below and does not score it.

**What an imported outline usually gets right, and may be kept:** the slide order, a per-slide format, the idea that a sent deck must stand alone, and headlines written as claims.

**What it usually gets wrong, and must be rerun:**

| Found in the outline | What to do with it |
|---|---|
| A number with no source: a tool bill, a share of time wasted, a market ladder | Tag it unsupported. Replace it with a placeholder, or cut it |
| A "validated" percentage gain | Cut it unless the founder measured it. If they did, record how |
| "First", "only", "the leading", or a universal negative such as "no tool can" | Run the prior-art check in section 6 |
| Price tiers | Check them against cost to serve (section 9). The founder decides |
| An ask with amount, milestone and revenue | Run the three checks in section 10 |
| Named pillars or branded names for parts of the product | Replace them with what each part does, in plain verbs |
| A capability stated as working | Check it against the claims ledger (section 3). If nobody has looked, its status is "stated, not checked" |
| A step, detail or capability the founder never said, or said differently | Compare each line with the founder's own words. Restore their wording or cut it |
| A product or company name, the founder's own included | Confirm it is the current name, on the owner's own page or in the company's documents |
| A map with the company alone in the best corner | Rebuild it under the conditions in the competition section of `slide-by-slide.md`, or drop it |

**Procedure:**

1. List every number and every claim in the outline.
2. Read the outline against the founder's own description, line by line, and mark anything that is not in it. An outline that reads better than the dictation has usually added something.
3. Tag each item: confirmed by the founder, sourced, an estimate with its inputs, or unsupported.
4. Turn each unsupported item into a placeholder, or cut it.
5. Run the arithmetic on the market, the price and the ask. Check that the same figure appears wherever it is repeated.
6. Check each capability against the ledger, and each name against its owner's page.
7. Tell the founder plainly what was removed, why, and what they now owe. Say what was kept as well.

**Numbers a reviewer proposes.** A reviewer, human or model, may say "put a $99 tier and a $399 tier on the slide" or "raise $2 million to reach 300 customers". That is advice about the form of the slide: it needs a price, and it needs an amount with a milestone. The advice about form is often right. The figures are illustrations. Run the three checks in section 10 on them, and check any starting count the proposal assumes, because a reviewer who writes "from 12 pilot shops" may have read a stage label as a customer count. Put the figures in front of the founder as proposals with the arithmetic shown, and print nothing until the founder sets the numbers. The same holds for any figure this skill suggests while reviewing.

## 13. The final gates

A deck is not final until every one of these holds. They are checks on the file, done by looking, not by trusting an earlier pass.

1. Every number on every slide has a row in the backup sheet, with its kind and its source or inputs.
2. No placeholder remains. Search the file for `[NEED`, `TBD`, `XX` and `lorem`. A hit means the deck is still a draft. It is not a reason to fill the slot.
3. Every ranking word and every universal claim about rivals has a prior-art note on file, or is gone.
4. Every statement about a named competitor has a first-party link and a date, and every product is called by the name it carries today.
5. The market sum, the price and the ask have been recomputed and agree with each other across slides.
6. Present tense matches the claims ledger, line by line. No capability shown on a slide is still "stated, not checked".
7. Sample screens carry "Sample data" and estimates carry "Estimate".
8. Every slide has been rendered and read, and the first text line of every page has been read in the file's text order (see Build mode, step 11, in `SKILL.md`).

**The backup sheet.** One row per number, kept with the deck, so the founder can answer the follow-up question:

| Slide | Number | Kind | Source or inputs | Date | Confirmed by |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 47 days to 12 days | Founder's own | Shop books, 310 invoices, March to August | [date] | Founder |
| 8 | 110,000 shops | Sourced | [publisher, table, year, link] | [date read] | n/a |
| 8 | $317 million a year | Estimate | 110,000 x $240 x 12 | [date] | Founder |
