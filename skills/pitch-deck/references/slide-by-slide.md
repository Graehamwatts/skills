# Slide by slide

Read this when writing the outline and again when writing each slide. In review mode, read the section for any slide you are about to mark down, so the fix you propose matches the slide's job.

Every company, person and figure in the examples is invented: Tallybird (invoice follow-up for small trade contractors) and Greenhatch (weekly ordering for independent plant nurseries). The numbers show what a filled-in slide looks like. Never reuse one. In a real draft, a number the founder has not given is a placeholder such as `[NEED: days to payment at the founder's own shop]`. The why-now examples use slots in square brackets for outside facts. The market examples use invented shop counts (110,000 and 6,200) so the arithmetic can be shown. They are not real statistics.

## Budgets by deck kind

These are working budgets. They exist because a slide that fits them can be read at the speed its reader reads.

| | Sent ahead, read alone | Attached to an application | Presented live |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main slides | 10 to 15, plus a short appendix | 6 to 8 | Set by the slot: about 5 for 3 minutes, 8 to 10 for 7, 12 to 15 for 15 |
| Headline | A claim of 8 to 12 words | The same | A claim of 4 to 8 words |
| Body | Three or four rows: a label of 2 to 4 words, then one plain line. No paragraphs | The same | One number or one picture. The rest is spoken |
| Body words | About 40 to 70 | The same | 15 or fewer |
| Speaker note | What the founder would say, 50 words at most | The same | Prompts: the one point, an opening line, the bridge to the next slide |
| A slide passes when | It makes sense forwarded to a stranger with no other slide | The same | It cannot be understood without the speaker, and reads in three seconds |

No topic takes more than three slides. Each of the three to five points the founder most wants remembered gets a slide of its own.

Three rules apply to every slide below.

- **No category tag over the headline, at any size.** "The Problem" above a claim adds nothing the slide order has not said, and it is the first line in the file's text order, so a model or a screening tool reads it as the title. A tag that carries something the headline lacks, such as a product name or "Appendix", may stay, smaller than the body text.
- **One figure may be larger than the headline, when it is the slide's visual.** A skimmer reads it first. So it must be the number the headline is about, show its unit and its kind in the same glance, and not be mistakable for a stronger kind of proof.
- **Text inside a screenshot or a mock screen is part of the visual.** It does not count against the body budget.

## The standard orders

A website sells the product to a customer. A deck argues a company to a reviewer. Use a pitch order, never the order of the company's website.

### Seed investor, sent ahead

1. Cover: what the company makes, for whom, and who built it
2. Problem
3. Solution
4. What changed (keep only if each shift has a date and a source)
5. The example, flagship product first
6. A second example (optional, never more than three in total)
7. The advantage
8. Proof and who built it
9. Market
10. Competition
11. Business model and price
12. How we reach customers
13. The ask
14. Close (optional)

Then the appendix.

**Move the proof up when it is the strongest thing in the deck.** Real revenue or real usage can sit third or fourth, ahead of the market. Put its two hardest facts in one line on the cover as well.

### Application reviewer

For an accelerator, a grant or a credit program. This reader works through a stack, reads alone and fast, and is deciding whether this is a real product company and where to route it. Six to eight slides.

1. Cover: what it is, who builds it, the stage, the two hardest facts
2. Problem
3. Solution, with one real screen and a link to the working product
4. The example
5. Proof and who built it, with the dated strip. This is the slide the reader decides on
6. Who pays, and how the company charges, in one sentence each
7. The ask, in the program's own terms
8. The advantage (optional)

Cut the market ladder, the why-now slide and the route to customers, or shrink each to a line. Leave round terms out of a deck that goes to people who are not investors. Answer what the application asks, and keep every number identical to the investor deck.

### Live pitch: 3, 7 and 15 minutes

Build the live deck as its own piece. Deleting words from the sent deck does not produce it.

| Slot | Where it happens | What fits |
|---|---|---|
| 3 minutes | Pitch competitions, demo days | Three things only: the opportunity, the proof, the advantage. About five slides: what we do, the problem, the example, the proof, the request for a meeting. Slow down. Do not pack more in |
| 7 minutes | Short investor meetings, showcases | Add the solution, the model, the route to customers and a little market. Eight to ten slides |
| 15 minutes | A full meeting booked for thirty or more | The whole argument, with the rest of the time left for questions. Twelve to fifteen slides |

Open on a statement about the company, not an introduction of the speaker. The audience can read the name on the title slide. Take questions before the ask, so that the ask is the last thing heard.

### Short variants

- **Five-slide follow-up.** For a reply to a cold message that earned permission to send more: what we do, the problem, the example, the proof, the ask.
- **One-page summary.** The cover line, three facts each with its source, and the ask. Text, not a slide image, so it can be forwarded and searched.
- **Later stages.** This skill is built for pre-seed and seed. A Series A deck has to survive diligence: proof moves to third, and twelve or more months of history and the cost of winning a customer become the center of the deck. Use this file for craft and say plainly that the stage needs more than it covers.

## One slide, written out

Keep the deck's words in one outline file, apart from whatever renders the slides, so wording can change without touching layout. One block per slide:

```
## Slide 2
Headline: A plumbing shop waits 47 days to collect on a finished job
Rows:
- The job is done: the crew leaves, and the invoice goes out by email a week later
- Nobody chases: the office manager runs payroll, permits and the phones
- The money sits: 31 of every 100 invoices pass 60 days unpaid
- The shop pays anyway: wages and materials go out before the customer pays
Visual: a real aged-invoice list from the founder's shop, names removed
Source: the founder's shop books, 310 invoices, March to August
Note: I ran that office for eleven years. Every Friday I chose between chasing money and getting trucks out. The work was finished. The money was not in. (Takeaway: the pain is a daily one and I have lived it.)
```

## The slide types

### 1. Cover

**Job.** A stranger knows what the company makes and for whom before reading anything else.

**It carries.** One plain line built from verbs, which is the headline. The name and logo, small. The founder and their standing in one line. A small stage label. Contact details. The two hardest facts, if the proof is strong.

**The tagline.** The founder's approved tagline may sit on the cover as a small line under the verbs line, or on the close. It is never the largest line. A slogan tells a stranger how the company wants to sound. The verbs line tells them what it makes.

**Headline patterns.**
- [Company] [verb] [customer] [result]
- [Kind of product] that [does what] for [whom]

| Good | Bad | Why the bad one fails |
|---|---|---|
| Tallybird gets small trade contractors paid in days, not weeks | Tallybird: The Future of Receivables | A slogan and a category crown. It names no customer and nothing the product does |
| Greenhatch turns a two-hour weekly order call into a ten-minute checklist | Reimagining how nurseries grow | The reader cannot picture the product |
| | Tallybird | A name alone tells a stranger nothing |

**Proof it needs.** None beyond being true. The stage label must be exact. It must not leave a reader thinking there are paying customers when there are none.

**Visual.** One real product screen, or the logo on the company's own look. Nothing else.

**Usual mistakes.** A slogan as the only line. A product name the reader has not been given. A category word in place of a description. Dates, "confidential" stamps and version numbers competing with the line.

**With no revenue.** The stage label says where things stand in positive, exact words: "Pre-launch. In daily use at one shop since March." Never the word "pre-revenue" alone, which says only what is missing.

### 2. Problem

**Job.** Show how the trouble lands on real people today, so the reader nods.

**Headline patterns.**
- [Customer] lose [amount] every [period] to [cause]
- [Customer] still [old way], and it costs them [cost]

| Good | Bad | Why the bad one fails |
|---|---|---|
| A plumbing shop waits 47 days to collect on a finished job | The Problem | A label. The skimmer learns nothing |
| Independent nurseries reorder stock by phone and fax every single week | Late payment is a $3 trillion problem | A statistic in place of a pain. Nobody feels a trillion, and it has no source |
| | Legacy receivables workflows are fragmented | Jargon. No customer has ever said this |

**Proof it needs.** The founder's own experience with a count, or a real customer's words used with permission, or one sourced number with its source line. Three or four statements nobody would dispute.

**Visual.** One real artifact of the pain: the aged-invoice list, the whiteboard, the call log.

**Usual mistakes.** Two problem slides. A statement the reader can argue with. Attacking competitors here. Starting to describe the solution.

**With no revenue.** Nothing changes. This slide needs no customers. The founder's time in the trade, with numbers, is the proof.

### 3. Solution

**Job.** Say what the product does and what the customer gets, in plain verbs.

**Headline patterns.**
- [Product] [does the thing] so [customer] [does not have to]
- [Product] turns [old effort] into [new effort]

| Good | Bad | Why the bad one fails |
|---|---|---|
| Tallybird sends every reminder so the office manager does not | Our Solution | A label |
| | An AI-powered, end-to-end receivables platform | Four consultant words and no verb. It could describe any product |
| | Three pillars: Engage, Automate, Reconcile | Invented names for the parts. The reader has to decode them |

**Rows.** Three or four things the product does, each answering a line on the problem slide. Outcomes for the customer, not a feature catalog.

**Proof it needs.** Every row is true under the claims ledger. A real screen.

**Visual.** One clear real screenshot. Make the product understandable in thirty seconds.

**Usual mistakes.** Technology first. A list of every feature. Present tense for something still being built.

**With no revenue.** Show what the product does. A capability still in build appears as what the product is made to do, its screen marked "Sample data", with no result attached. Runs today: "Tallybird texts the customer." Being built: "Tallybird is built to call after three texts." The label on the screen does not cover a present-tense sentence beside it.

### 4. What changed

**Job.** Say why this could not be built or sold a few years ago.

**Keep the slide only if every shift on it has a date and a source.** Otherwise cut it. A vague why-now is worse than none.

Dates and sources for these shifts are research, not a question for the founder. Look them up when a search tool is available, and record the publisher's own page. Cut any shift you cannot date, and cut any shift that helps every competitor as much as it helps this company.

**Headline patterns.**
- [Specific thing] became [cheap, possible or required] in [year]
- Since [year], [customers] [new habit], which makes [product] possible

| Good | Bad | Why the bad one fails |
|---|---|---|
| A text with a pay link now costs [price], down from [price] in [year] | Why Now | A label |
| Since [year], [share] of homeowners pay contractors from a phone | AI is transforming every industry | True of every deck written this year. It is not about this product |
| | The market is at an inflection point | No date, no source, nothing to check |

**Proof it needs.** Up to three shifts: a technology, a price, a rule, a habit. Each has a date and a first-party source line.

**Visual.** A short timeline with the two or three dated shifts.

**Usual mistakes.** Trends with no dates. A shift that helps every competitor equally and says nothing about this company.

**With no revenue.** The same. In an application deck, cut this slide.

### 5. The example: old way against new way

**Job.** This is the slide where the product clicks. It is told the way a person explains something: let me give you an example.

**Rules.** One to three examples, never four. The product the founder calls the flagship comes first. Each example is one real case of the founder's, shown the old way beside the new way.

One exception to flagship first. If only another product has a real, dated case with a measured result, lead with that case, put the flagship second, and tell the founder why. Ask for a real flagship case so the order can go back.

**Headline patterns.**
- One [real case]: [old result] before, [new result] with [product]
- Last [month], [what happened], in [time] where it used to take [time]

| Good | Bad | Why the bad one fails |
|---|---|---|
| One $8,400 boiler job got paid in 9 days after three texts | Use Case | A label |
| | Tallybird saves contractors up to 80% of their time | Nobody measured it, and "up to" gives that away |
| | How Tallybird streamlines reminders, reporting, payments and reconciliation | Four features merged into one example. None of them lands |

**Layout.** Left: the old way, with its steps, how long it took and what went wrong. Right: the new way, with its steps, how long it took and the result. The same case on both sides.

**Proof it needs.** The case is real and dated. It comes from the founder's own use or from a named user who agreed. A time saving is measured, or marked "Estimate" with its inputs on file.

**Visual.** The side-by-side is the visual. A real screen sits on the new-way side.

**Usual mistakes.** "Imagine a contractor who..." in place of a real case. An invented saving. A tour of features dressed as an example. A fourth example.

**With no revenue.** The founder's own operation is the case. If the product has not yet run on a real case, show the old way from the founder's records and the new way as designed, with "Sample data" on the screen and no result claimed.

### 6. The advantage

**Job.** Name the one thing that is hard to copy, and give it a slide of its own with the same visual weight as the examples.

The company's strongest long-term advantage is often the thing most likely to be buried as one bullet in a list. A headline that counts things, such as "four reasons shops stay", is the sign that it has been. So is a headline that names the advantage above a body that splits into three equal rows about other things. Pull it out, and give it the whole body.

Two questions test it. Why can an established company with the same tools not follow? What grows with every customer? "We started earlier", "we are faster" and "our interface is nicer" answer neither.

**Headline patterns.**
- We know [specific thing] because [how we came by it]
- Every [job or customer] adds [asset], and [why that matters]

| Good | Bad | Why the bad one fails |
|---|---|---|
| We know which reminder works because we chased 310 invoices by hand | Our Moat | A label, and a consultant word |
| | Proprietary AI and a best-in-class team | Nothing a reader can check or picture |
| | Four reasons shops stay | A count. The real point is hidden inside a list |

**Proof it needs.** How the founder came by it, with a count. If the capability behind it is still being built, show what it is made to do and attach no result.

**Visual.** One diagram of the thing itself: the loop from reminder to payment, or the founder's log.

**Usual mistakes.** Three unrelated points sharing the slide. Naming it with a consultant word. Claiming an advantage any funded competitor could buy.

**With no revenue.** The founder's knowledge of the trade is often the advantage. Say it with its numbers. Being in the trade is common. The numbers are the credential.

### 7. Proof and who built it

**Job.** Show that this team gets real things done, and quickly.

**Headline patterns.**
- [Number] [kind of proof] in [time]
- Built in [time] by [who]
- At [where], [measure] went from [before] to [after]

| Good | Bad | Why the bad one fails |
|---|---|---|
| At our own shop, payment time fell from 47 days to 12 | Traction | A label |
| Built in five months by an office manager and one engineer | Strong early traction and growing demand | Adjectives. No number, no date |
| | 500+ on the waitlist! | A waitlist alone is not proof of demand |
| | Validated 3x return for customers | Nobody measured it, and there are no customers |

**With revenue or real usage.** Lead with the strongest number. Put a date and a time window on it. Show growth beside the absolute figure. Start the chart at zero. Label each count by its kind of proof (see `evidence-rules.md`, section 5).

**With no revenue and no outside users.** Traction becomes progress: what has been done since the start, and how long it took. Use what is true from this list.

1. **The clock.** A real start date, today's date, and what was built between them.
2. **Anything hard to get that the company holds**: a license, a data agreement, a certification, with how long it took.
3. **The founder as the first user**, with one count from real use. Without a count it is a statement, not evidence.
4. **The founder's record in the trade**: years, volume, one true measure.
5. **Pace, and who builds.** Releases since a date. Who writes the product.
6. **Results on little money**, if the founder has paid for it so far.
7. **Signed letters of intent**, with their value and terms, if any exist.
8. **A strip of Done, Now, Next with a month on each.** This is the one place the stage is spelled out.

Never on this slide: a waitlist alone, a pipeline, talks in progress, a future date presented as a result, accelerator logos, advisors, a gain nobody measured, the word "validated" over an estimate. And never a line about what the company does not have. "No revenue yet" is true, and it is an answer for the question period. The slide stays honest by claiming nothing false, not by listing gaps.

**Visual.** The dated strip. One chart if there is real data to chart. If one figure is set large, it is the product's own number.

**Usual mistakes.** Hand-picked time windows. Cumulative charts. Signups presented as customers. Progress with no dates. A founder's career total set as the largest text, where it reads as the company's result. Label it as theirs and keep it smaller than the product's proof.

A reader at this stage is watching for the first paying customer with no tie to the founder. That is the milestone the ask should buy.

### 8. Team, when it gets its own slide

At seed the team usually shares a slide with the proof, as "who built it". Give it a slide of its own only when the team is the main reason to believe.

**Headline patterns.**
- [Founder] [did the relevant thing] for [how long] before building this

| Good | Bad | Why the bad one fails |
|---|---|---|
| Our founder ran a plumbing office for eleven years before building this | Team | A label |
| | A world-class team with decades of combined experience | A ranking word and a sum of years. It names nobody's achievement |

**It carries.** The people who do the work. One line of relevant achievement each. Why this team for this problem. The gap in the team, and the hire this round pays for.

**It never carries.** Advisors. A wall of former-employer logos. Full biographies, which go in the appendix.

Years spent inside the trade count as proof. So does having built and shipped the product.

### 9. Market

**Job.** Show how big this gets, as a sum the founder can say out loud.

**Headline patterns.**
- [Count] [customers] would each pay [price] a [period]
- We start with [count] [customers] in [first segment]

| Good | Bad | Why the bad one fails |
|---|---|---|
| 110,000 small trade shops would each pay $240 a month | Market Size | A label |
| | A $50B total addressable market | A lifted total. No working, no source, no year |
| | Capturing just 1% makes us a $500M company | The one percent claim. Every reader has seen it and none believe it |

**It carries.** The sum written out: 110,000 x $240 x 12 = about $317 million a year. The first segment: 6,200 plumbing shops in two states, about $17.9 million a year. What comes after the first segment. A source line under each count. The word "Estimate" beside each product.

**Visual.** The sum itself, large. Not three nested rings.

**Usual mistakes.** Rings with no arithmetic. Neighboring markets added in. Data with no year. Money that passes through the industry, presented as money the customer would pay.

**With no revenue.** The same slide. Until a price is set, show the customer count and what each spends today on what the product replaces, as two facts, not multiplied.

**In an application deck.** Who pays first, in one line. No dollar market size.

### 10. Competition

**Job.** Show that the founder knows what the customer uses today, and why there is still room.

The competitor is everything else the customer could spend time, attention and money on, doing nothing included. Being better does not mean they buy. The slide has to explain why the customer has not already solved this with what they have.

**The default form: alternatives by kind.** Three or four rows, one per kind of alternative, each with what that kind leaves undone:

- Doing it by hand, or not doing it
- The tool the customer already pays for
- A hired person or an outside service
- Products built for this job

Then the answer to the question every reader asks: will the established company not simply add this?

**Headline patterns.**
- [Customers] [still do it the old way] because [existing tools stop at X]
- [Established tools] can add this. [The specific thing] is what is hard for them

| Good | Bad | Why the bad one fails |
|---|---|---|
| Shops chase invoices by hand because accounting tools stop at the reminder | Competition | A label |
| | We have no direct competitors | It reads as not having looked, or as no market |
| | A grid where Tallybird has every tick and each rival has gaps | See below |

**Why a tick grid loses the reader.** Every founder draws one in which they win every row, so readers discount it on sight. The founder chose the rows, so it proves only that the founder can choose rows. It compares features, when the real question is why the customer has not solved the problem already. One wrong cell about a company the reader knows discredits the slide. And it is a ranking claim in picture form, with no evidence behind it.

**What counts as a tick grid.** Any table in which the company's column is all ticks or all filled while every other column has blanks, whatever the other cells say. Columns named by kind of alternative, sourced text in the rivals' cells and honest blanks do not change it. The reader still sees one full column and several broken ones.

**A two-by-two map is not automatically better.** A map with the company alone in the top right corner is the same move as the tick grid. A map works only when all four of these hold:

1. The two axes are things a customer would name when choosing, and each can be read off public pages. For Tallybird: who does the chasing (the shop or the software), and how the customer is reached (email only, or text and phone).
2. Every placement cites the competitor's own page and the date it was read.
3. A competitor that describes itself the way you describe yourself is drawn where its page puts it, even when that is beside you.
4. The product really does stand apart on those axes. If two rivals share your corner, the map is not your slide. Use the alternatives-by-kind form.

Choose the axes the buyer uses to choose. Never choose the axes on which you win.

**Concede, then claim.** Answer "an established company can build that" in three moves:

1. **Concede what is true.** "Accounting tools can add text reminders, and one lists them as planned ([their changelog, date read])."
2. **Name the specific thing that is hard for them, and why.** "Their product is built for the bookkeeper, who sends invoices once a month. Chasing needs what the crew knows: who signed off, and when the customer said they would pay. We collect that at the job site."
3. **Say what would have to be true in twelve months for the claim to hold.** "Shops on Tallybird should still be collecting faster than shops using the accounting tool's own reminders. We will measure it with our first 40 shops."

If the founder does not have move 2 yet, leave a placeholder: `[NEED: the one thing that is hard for an established company, founder]`. Offer at most one candidate, labeled as a candidate to test. Do not write the reason for them. A reason the founder cannot defend out loud is worse than a gap in a draft.

**Named competitors go in the appendix**, in a table where every row has a first-party source and the date read. The main slide speaks of kinds.

**Visual.** The rows by kind, or a map that meets the four conditions.

**With no revenue.** The same slide.

### 11. Business model and price

**Job.** Say how the company makes money, with a price on the slide.

**Headline patterns.**
- One price of [amount] a [period] replaces [what the customer pays for today]
- [Customers] pay [amount] per [unit], plus [usage]

| Good | Bad | Why the bad one fails |
|---|---|---|
| One price of $240 a month replaces a part-time collections hire | Business Model | A label |
| | Flexible pricing built for every team | Says nothing. It reads as not having decided |
| | Three tiers nobody has costed | An invented price is an invented number |

**It carries.**
- One model in one sentence, the model the field already uses.
- A price or a range, set by the founder. A final price is not expected. The order of magnitude is. A blank reads as not knowing.
- What it replaces: the customer's bill today, people as well as software, each line tagged as sourced, the founder's or an estimate. People are often the larger part of that bill.
- Whose budget it comes from: an existing line, or new money the customer must find. Replacing a line is the easier sale.

**It never carries.** A menu of possible models. A multi-year forecast. A tier that was not checked against cost to serve (see `evidence-rules.md`, section 9).

**Visual.** The price, large, beside today's bill. If one figure on this slide is larger than the headline, it is the price, not a saving or an hours estimate.

**With no revenue.** The slide still needs a price. A price is a decision, not a result. If the founder has not decided, it is one of the few questions worth asking (see `founder-interview.md`). Bring a worked range when the cost and the replaced spend are on file. When they are not, ask for those two inputs. Until the founder sets it, the slide holds a placeholder.

**In an application deck.** How the company charges, in one sentence.

### 12. How we reach customers

**Job.** Show the one concrete route to the first outside customers, run by the founder.

**Headline patterns.**
- Our first [number] [customers] come through [specific channel]

| Good | Bad | Why the bad one fails |
|---|---|---|
| Our first 40 shops come through two plumbing supply counters | Go-To-Market Strategy | A label |
| | Content, paid search, partnerships, referrals and viral growth | A brainstorm. Six channels means none has been tried |

**Proof it needs.** Why this route: the founder's existing relationships, with a count. The steps, with dates. Who does each one.

**Visual.** The route in three steps.

**Usual mistakes.** A list of every channel. An untested plan stated as a certainty. No named owner.

**With no revenue.** This slide matters more, not less. It is the plan for reaching the milestone in the ask.

### 13. The ask

**Job.** Say what you want and what it gets you to. It is the last slide, and it is swappable, so the same core deck serves an investor, a program and an accelerator.

**If raising, it carries:**

1. The amount, as one figure and not a range, and the instrument.
2. What it buys: a countable milestone with a date.
3. The runway in months, reaching the point where the company can raise again or live on revenue.
4. Who has committed, if anyone has.
5. The next step you want: a further conversation. Not the check. Nobody invests off a deck, so ask for something the reader can say yes to.
6. Arithmetic that reconciles (see `evidence-rules.md`, section 10).

**Headline patterns.**
- We are raising [amount] to reach [milestone] in [months]

| Good | Bad | Why the bad one fails |
|---|---|---|
| We are raising $900,000 to reach 150 paying shops in 18 months | The Ask | A label |
| | We are opening our seed round | No amount, no milestone, nothing to respond to |
| | Raising $1M to $3M to accelerate growth | A range, and "growth" is not a milestone |

**If not raising, or writing to a program.** Three specific requests in the reader's own terms, what they let the company reach, and by when: "We ask for $25,000 in credits to run 30 pilot nurseries." No round terms.

**On a public stage.** Ask for the meeting. Whether a raise amount may be shown in public is a legal question, so the founder should check with their lawyer before putting one on a stage slide.

**Visual.** The amount, large. The milestone. Three or four use-of-funds lines that add up. The runway.

**Usual mistakes.** No number. A range. A list of hires in place of a milestone. Percentages that do not sum. Asking for the check. Ending on "any questions".

**With no revenue.** The milestone is the first paying customers with no tie to the founder.

The amount, the instrument and the milestone are the founder's decisions. Never fill them in for them.

### 14. Close

Optional. The three or four points worth remembering stay on screen with the contact details: "Tallybird gets contractors paid in 12 days, and 150 shops are next." The founder's tagline can sit here as a small line.

Never end on a bare "Thank you", "Q&A" or "Appendix". The last slide showing is the one that stays up longest.

### 15. Appendix

**Job.** Hold the answers to questions someone is likely to ask.

**It carries.**
- Named competitors, each with a first-party source and the date read
- The cost-to-serve working behind the price
- The market working and its sources
- Detail behind the Done, Now, Next strip
- Exact answers to the hard questions: revenue to date, users to date, what is not built yet
- The financial model, full biographies, architecture and compliance, where they exist

**The objection sheet.** Keep it with the appendix, for the founder and not for the reader. List at least five objections this deck is likely to draw. For each one write what on the deck triggers it, the answer the founder would say out loud in two or three sentences, and any change to the deck that would head it off. The usual sources of objections: the market is too small, a bigger company will do it, the team has a gap, the price will not hold, the timing is wrong, the first customers will be hard to reach.

Negatives and objections live here and in the speaker notes, written out and ready. They are answers, not slide text. A line on a main slide about what the company does not do spends space on something one question would settle.

**It never carries.** Anything the main argument depends on.

Appendix pages are reference pages, so a plain label works as a title there. Mark clearly where the appendix starts.
