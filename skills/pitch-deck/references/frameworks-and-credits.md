## 1. The frameworks

### Y Combinator

Y Combinator's partners have published more advice on early-stage decks than anyone, across essays and talks. The parts this skill leans on: say what the company makes in one plain sentence before anything else (Michael Seibel, "How to Pitch Your Company"); use a short seed template that opens on title, problem and solution, puts traction fourth, gives the founder's insight a slide of its own and ends on the ask (Aaron Harris, "How to build your seed round pitch deck", which has no competition slide and no why-now slide); make slides legible, simple and obvious to a stranger (Kevin Hale, "How to Design a Better Pitch Deck"); and when there is no revenue, show progress against a clock (Michael Seibel, "How To Perfectly Pitch Your Seed Stage Startup With Y Combinator's Michael Seibel", a SaaStr talk). From these come this skill's plain cover line, one point per slide, market size built from customers times price, a date beside every progress number, and the proof slide for a company with no revenue.

- https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4b-how-to-pitch-your-company
- https://www.ycombinator.com/library/2u-how-to-build-your-seed-round-pitch-deck
- https://www.ycombinator.com/library/4T-how-to-design-a-better-pitch-deck
- https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lw2X3PxKlAY

### Sequoia

Sequoia Capital's "Writing a Business Plan" (an undated page) is a ten-part outline: company purpose, problem, solution, why now, market potential, competition or alternatives, business model, team, financials, vision. It is the usual source of the why-now slide, and it frames competition as alternatives, which is the form this skill uses by default. This skill adds one condition Sequoia does not state: a why-now slide stays only when each shift on it has a date and a source.

- https://www.sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan/

### Slidebean

Slidebean has published two widely watched walk-throughs. The 2018 video, "How to Make a Pitch Deck for Investors", teaches a slide order with a two-axis competition map, market size built from the bottom up, a team slide limited to the people doing the work, and proof placed late in a sent deck and early on a stage. Its newer guide, uploaded in 2024, revises several of those points: stop counting slides and answer a few core questions, open on what the company does in one plain line, state one business model, describe the single way the first customers arrive in place of a brainstorm of channels, and leave video out of the file because decks are read on phones. Where the two differ, this skill follows the later one. It does not follow the 2018 advice to present an untested route to customers as if it were certain.

- https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SB16xgtFmco
- https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jDPsZM82hC0

### The wedge

The idea comes from David Sacks's 2019 essays, chiefly "The Sharp Startup: When PayPal Found Product-Market Fit" and "The Wilderness Period". A young company gets in through one sharp product or use that drives the sale to one specific first customer, then expands from there. A bundle of weak uses is worse than one strong one. He also sets a bar for leaving the earliest stage: revenue from people with no tie to the founder. In this skill the idea decides three things: the flagship product comes first, the market slide names a first segment before the total, and the ask buys the first paying customers with no tie to the founder. The word itself stays in the working notes and off the slides.

- https://sacks.substack.com/p/the-sharp-startup-when-paypal-found-product-market-fit-5ba47ad35d0b
- https://sacks.substack.com/p/the-wilderness-period-61f009c769ac

### The send-ahead deck and the skim

Lance Cottrell's Feel the Boot channel makes the clearest case that one deck cannot do two jobs. In "The Pitch Deck Mistake Every Founder Makes" (2025) he lists the different pitches a founder needs and singles out the emailed, self-guided deck as the one most often missing: it carries the explanation a speaker would give, with no paragraphs, and it is first read as a fast skim, so each headline has to state the slide's point in place of a title such as "problem" or "solution". "Make Two Pitch Decks: Presentation and Email" (2019) adds that each version should be built as its own piece, sharing one look and one vocabulary. His related videos shaped this skill's ask slide (lay out the deal and what it delivers, then ask for the next step and not the check), its competition slide (the competitor is everything else the customer could spend on, and the top-right corner and the all-ticks table are the standard founder move), and its advice on opening a spoken pitch. Haje Jan Kamps makes the same two-documents case in "You may need more than one pitch deck" (TechCrunch, 2022).

Two honest notes. "Skim test" is a label used here for the idea. It is not a phrase from the videos, which say that the first read is a skim and the deck must inform on it. And the videos say the headline should state the point in place of a category title. They do not rule on a small tag above a claim. Dropping every category tag, at any size, is this skill's own rule. It comes from testing: in a PDF's text order the tag is the first line of the page, and a model that reviewed a tested deck read 13 point tags as the titles.

## 2. Where the frameworks disagree, and what this skill does

| Question | The disagreement | This skill |
|---|---|---|
| One deck or several | Some YC advice says one simple deck can be sent and presented. Feel the Boot and Kamps say two different documents | Build the sent deck first, then make the live version as its own piece |
| Titles: claim or label | Older published decks use labels. Current teaching uses claims | Claims. The largest line on a slide is its point, and no category tag sits above it |
| Competition slide | Absent from the YC seed template. Present in Sequoia's outline. A two-axis map in Slidebean's 2018 video | One slide by kind of alternative in a sent investor deck, named competitors in the appendix, a map only under four conditions |
| Market number | Some advice says no number is needed. Most asks for arithmetic from the bottom up | The sum, written out, with a named first segment. No rings |
| Why now | In Sequoia's outline. Not in the YC seed template | Kept only with dates and sources. Cut from application decks |
| Where proof goes | Fourth in the YC template. Late in a sent deck and first on a stage in Slidebean's 2018 video | Before market and competition. Earlier when it is the strongest thing. Its two hardest facts on the cover |
| Naming the limits | YC advice says to name a weakness before the reader finds it. Other advice says never spend a slide on what might go wrong | The stage is said once, in positive and exact words. Nothing false is claimed. Hard answers are written out in the appendix and the notes |
| A price on the slide | YC advice wants a price, even a provisional one. Some founders prefer none | A price or a range set by the founder, checked against cost to serve |

When a source's rule and the truth rules collide, the truth rules win. When a source's rule and the founder's ruling on tone collide, the founder's ruling wins, as long as nothing false is claimed.

## 6. How the sources were used

- **Ideas, not wording.** Slide orders, checklists and tests are ideas, and ideas are free to reuse. The wording is this skill's own, apart from the one list heading credited in the table above.
- **Examples are invented.** None comes from a source or from a real company. Each invented company name was searched before use.
- **The scripts are original.** `scripts/skim_test.py` and `scripts/deck_inventory.py` share no code with any scorer studied.
- **Lessons from practice.** Several rules come from building one first-time founder's deck through six versions: the tone rules, the claims ledger, the short interview, the handling of imported outlines, and verifying by looking. Those notes name no person, company or figure, and neither does this skill.
- **Lessons from testing.** Other rules come from running this skill on a real deck and a real imported outline: the text-order read, the rule on text set larger than the headline, the rule that a deck for investors with no price or no definite ask is not ready, the "stated, not checked" status, the check that a product name is current, and Import mode. They are this skill's own judgment, not a source's.
- **Errors are this skill's own.** If a summary above misstates a source, the source is right.
