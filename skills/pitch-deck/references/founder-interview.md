# The founder interview

Read this before asking the founder anything, in any mode: build, review or import.

The example message at the end uses an invented company. Never reuse its numbers.

## 1. Why the interview is short

A founder who is asked something their own documents already answer concludes that nobody read the documents. They are right, and the rest of the work starts from a deficit.

So the interview has one job: get the things that exist only in the founder's head. Their numbers. Their dates. The decisions they have not made yet. Everything else is read, researched, or stated as an assumption they can correct in a word.

A long questionnaire also produces worse answers. Fifteen questions get fifteen thin replies. Five get real ones.

## 2. Read first

Read all of it before the first question. Not the first page of each.

- The website, every page, pricing and terms included.
- The product: use it if there is access. Otherwise screenshots, a recording or a demo.
- Product documentation, help pages, specifications and the roadmap.
- Earlier decks, one-pagers and any application already submitted.
- The founder's own notes, voice memos, dictation and call transcripts.
- Bills and cost records, if they are shared.
- The company's look: its site, its product interface, its design files. The deck should look like the company, so find this now.
- The public pages of the alternatives the founder names.

If the founder has given nothing but a name, send one message asking for the material and wait (section 6).

## 3. Sort what you found

Put everything into three piles before writing a question.

| Pile | Where it goes | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Known, with where it came from | The facts sheet and the claims ledger | "Start date: March, from the founder's notes" |
| Assumed | A statement to put to the founder | "I am treating the reminders product as the flagship" |
| Unknown, and only the founder can know | The question list | "What does one shop cost you to serve in a month?" |

Anything that can be looked up belongs in none of the three as a question. Look it up. How many customers exist, what a competitor's page says, what a rule requires: these are research, and the result goes on the facts sheet with its source.

## 4. What only the founder can know, by slide

This is a bank to choose from, not a list to send. Most decks need five or fewer of these, because the documents answer the rest.

**The deck itself**
- Who is this going to, how will they get it (emailed, presented, attached to an application), and by when?

**Cover**
- How do you describe the company out loud to someone in your own trade?
- Is there a tagline you have already approved?

**Problem**
- In your own operation, how often did this happen, how long did it take, and what did it cost? Your number, not the industry's.

**The example**
- Which real case would you tell over dinner? Which job or customer, when, what happened the old way, and what happened with the product?
- Which product do you think of as the flagship?

**The advantage**
- What do you know about this work that other people building for it do not? How did you come to know it?

**Proof**
- When did work start, and who builds the product?
- What runs today, and what is still being built? Ask this to confirm the claims ledger, not to fill it from nothing.
- What is one count from your own real use?
- Who uses it today outside the company? Do they pay?
- Is anything signed?

**Market**
- Who buys first? Which exact kind of customer, and where?

**Competition**
- What do your customers use today?
- When a customer hears about you, which other name comes up?

**Business model and price**
- What will you charge? If that is not decided, see section 5.
- What does one customer cost you to serve in a month? Which bills is that built from?
- What does the customer stop paying for when they buy this?

**How we reach customers**
- How will the first ten customers outside your own circle arrive? Which relationships exist already?

**The ask**
- How much, on what instrument, to reach what, in how many months?
- Has anyone committed?
- What do you want this particular reader to do next?

**Tone**
- Which words would you never use about your own company?
- Is there anything that must not be said?

## 5. How to ask

**One message. Five questions or fewer. Numbered.** The founder should be able to answer from a phone.

**Open with what you read and what you understood.** Three to five lines. It shows the reading was done, and it lets the founder correct a wrong reading before it reaches a slide.

**State assumptions as statements.** Write "I am treating this as a deck you email ahead of a first meeting", not "Is this a deck you will email?". A statement costs the founder nothing when it is right and one word when it is wrong.

**Order the questions by how much the answer changes the deck.** The usual order: the deck kind and reader, the flagship example, the price, the ask, the count from the founder's own use.

**For a decision that has not been made, bring a range and ask them to pick.**
- Price: the floor comes from cost to serve. The ceiling comes from what the customer pays today for what the product replaces. Show both and the working.
- The ask: months of runway, times the monthly spend that reaches the milestone. Show the sum.
- If the inputs are not on file, ask for the inputs in one question: the bills behind one customer's monthly cost, and what the customer pays today for what the product replaces. Do not build a range from an imported outline's figures, or from competitors' price pages alone.

A founder choosing between two worked options answers in a minute. A founder asked "what is your price?" may not answer for a week. Whatever they pick is their number, and it goes on the facts sheet as theirs. Until they pick, the slide holds a placeholder.

**Ask for artifacts as well as answers.** One real screenshot, one real invoice or one page of the founder's own log improves a deck more than a paragraph of description.

**Record every answer on the facts sheet with its date.** The newest thing the founder says wins. Tell them when it replaces an earlier fact.

**Do not ask:**
- Anything the documents answer.
- Anything a search answers.
- Anything you can put as an assumption.
- Anything the founder has already said. Quote their answer back as a statement.
- Anything they have said they do not know. Bring a draft answer for them to correct.
- A founder with no revenue to explain why not. The deck does not need it, and the question does harm.

**The cap holds in every mode.** A review can list twenty things the deck leaves open. The message to the founder still carries five questions or fewer, chosen by how much each answer changes the deck.

## 6. When the founder says "just draft it"

Then draft it. Do not hold the work hostage to the interview.

- Print the assumptions at the top of the draft, each as a single statement.
- Put a placeholder wherever a number is missing. Never fill the slot.
- End the draft with **Numbers to find**: every placeholder, and who can supply it.

One limit. With no material at all, a company name and a request, there is nothing to draft from. Send one message that lists what you need, then wait. Do not write a deck from imagination. Do not send back a blank template either, since a template with empty slots is not a draft. The needed items are: what the company makes and for whom, what exists today, the founder's background in the field, who the deck is for, and what they are asking for.

## 7. When the founder dictates or rambles

Many founders think out loud, and a long dictated message is the best source there is. Mine it. Do not tidy it.

Pull out:
- **Every number.** Tag each as the founder's own, with the date they said it.
- **Every capability they describe, and the tense they use.** Each one gets a row in the claims ledger. "Will", "should be able to", "in theory" and "imagine" mean it does not run yet. That wording is evidence, and it outranks the present tense in any outline written from the same dictation.
- **Every real case they mention.** These are the candidates for the example slides. A case with a date and a measured result is worth more than the flagship with neither.
- **What they say they do not know.** "As far as I know" and "I am not sure this is accurate" mark a claim to check, not a question to send back to them.
- **Their phrases.** The words they use for their customers, their product and their problem are the deck's vocabulary. If they say "getting paid", the slide does not say "receivables optimization".
- **What they repeat.** A point made three times in one ramble is a point they want remembered.

Then play back the three to five points you think they most want a reader to remember, and ask them to rank them. That ranking decides which points get a slide of their own.

## 8. The second round: the three weakest claims

After the headline outline exists, pick the three claims the deck leans on hardest with the least support. Ask the founder four things about each:

1. What exactly are we claiming?
2. How do you know?
3. Which customer, case or number shows it?
4. What would a competitor get wrong about this?

Leave out any of the four that the founder has already answered. The second round is a message of its own, and it still holds to five questions: send the follow-ups whose answers would change a slide, and keep the rest for the notes.

Their answers decide what happens to each claim. A claim that gets more specific stays, and the detail goes into the speaker note or the appendix. A claim that gets vaguer is softened or cut.

This round is optional. Use it when the founder is engaged and the deck is going to a reader who will probe.

## 9. An example message

Invented company. This shows the shape: what was read, the assumptions, then five questions.

```
I have read the site, the product guide, the March one-pager and your two
voice notes. Here is how I understand it:

- Tallybird sends payment reminders for small trade contractors, by text,
  from the shop's own number.
- You ran the office at a plumbing shop for eleven years and built this
  with one engineer, starting last October.
- It has run at your old shop since March. The follow-up call feature is
  specified and not yet running.

I am assuming:
- This is a deck you will email to seed investors ahead of a first meeting.
- Reminders by text is the flagship, and the boiler job you mentioned is
  the example.
- Small plumbing shops in your two states are the first customers.

Five things only you can tell me:

1. Across the invoices Tallybird has chased at the shop, how many were
   there, and how many days to payment before and after?
2. Price. One shop costs about $42 a month to serve, from your messaging
   and hosting bills. A part-time collections person costs a shop far more
   than that. I would put the price between $180 and $280 a month. What is
   your number?
3. The ask: how much are you raising, and on what instrument? If it is not
   decided, tell me how many paying shops you want to reach and by when,
   and I will work out what that needs.
4. How do the first ten shops outside your own circle find you?
5. Can you send one real screenshot of the reminder thread from the boiler
   job, with the customer's name removed?

Correct anything above that I have wrong.
```
